Calculation order

The tool runs three installation gates first. If any gate fails, the set count is forced to zero regardless of how much the deck could carry, because a vessel that can transport components but cannot lift them into position is not usable for that turbine.

Gate 1: water depth

Available leg below hull = leg length − hull depth − leg reserve above main deck.
Required = water depth + tide and surge + air gap + leg penetration.

Gate 2: hook reach

Max hook above sea level = hook height above main deck + hull depth + air gap.
Required = hub height + rigging and tool height under the hook + clearance above the hub flange.

Gate 3: crane capacity

Heaviest lift = the largest of (nacelle + nacelle tool), (tower + tower tool), (blade + blade tool), each including its lifting tool. Compared against the crane capacity interpolated to the working radius from the two entered capacity and radius points.

Gate 4: deck strength (advisory)

Heaviest component divided by its seafastening footprint, against the deck strength. Flags a warning rather than forcing zero, because local load spreading is a detail design matter.

Deck capacity model

One set. One tower, one nacelle with the hub mounted on it, and three blades. A vessel carrying 6 towers carries 6 nacelles and 18 blades.

Coordinate convention. x along the centreline, bow at +x. y across the beam, +y to port, −y to starboard. The deck plan looks down, so port is at the top.

The arrangement is solved, not estimated. The tool places every component on the deck geometrically and increases the set count until something no longer fits. The number and the drawing come from the same routine, so they can never disagree. Clearances use separating-axis tests on the actual footprints rather than circumscribed circles, which over-exclude square seafastenings by roughly 40 percent.

Zones, aft to forward. Blade rack across the full beam, taking blades only. Crane tub, carrying nothing. The boom stows diagonally at a per-vessel angle, default 17°, and only the run that crosses the cargo deck shadows it; the rest overhangs the bow. Nacelles stow beneath that shadow but stay square to the hull rather than skewed to the boom, since seafastening runs on the ship's own grid. Upright towers can never go under the boom, so they stand clear of it. Nacelles that do not fit under the boom overflow onto open deck and compete with towers for the same ground. A reserve at the forward end holds containers and project kit, and the accommodation block and helideck sit forward of the forward legs.

Towers hug the sides. Tower positions are filled outboard first, working in from each rail, so the load runs into the side structure rather than the middle of the deck. Only once the rails are full does a tower move inboard.

Caps. The geometric arrangement, payload divided by set weight, and blade rack capacity. The lowest wins, and any failed installation gate overrides all of it with zero.

Component footprints

Seafastening areas already contain the safe gap between adjacent units, so the model packs them edge to edge with no further spacing. Anchored on measured 10 MW figures: 102 m² tower, 160 m² nacelle, 550 m² blade rack, 250 m² lifting tools. Other sizes scale by rotor diameter squared, which independently reproduces a 12.5 m square tower seafastening at 15 MW.

Calibration

Against the published figures the model returns 5 sets of 15 MW on Wind Maker against 5, four 14 MW sets on Wind Orca against 4, and three 15 MW sets on Wind Orca against 3. Wind Peak returns 6 against a published 7, one short.

These come out of the geometry rather than a tuned constant, which is the point. If a result looks wrong for a vessel you know, the fix is in that vessel's data, most likely boom shadow width, boom stow angle, accommodation length or crane base area, not in a global fudge factor.

Not modelled

Transit stability and VCG limits, which often bite before deck area does on a tall stow. Deck load spreading and grillage design. Crane slew interference between stowed cargo and the boom. Jacking cycle time and weather workability, which drive the economics rather than the capacity. Port constraints: quay draft, crane outreach at the marshalling harbour, air draft under bridges. Single blade versus bunny ear installation method. Blade root clearance during slew.

Internal engineering tool. Output is an estimate for screening and comparison, not a substitute for a stowage plan or a lift study.
